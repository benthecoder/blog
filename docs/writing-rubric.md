# Benedict's writing rubric

Version 1.0 · 7 November 2026

Use this when reviewing, grading, or revising a journal entry, personal essay, reading response, or explainer. Read the whole piece, choose its purpose, then assess the writing on the page. Finish with one manageable practice target.

This is a research-informed personal rubric. The criteria, five-point scale, weights, exercises, and review template below are local adaptations, not an official university rubric or a prediction of a professor's grade. A score describes this draft's effectiveness for its purpose; it does not grade the writer, the importance of the experience, or how much the writer disclosed.

## What the research contributes

- **Harvard: distinguish an accomplished essay from a merely correct one.** The College Writing Program's Fall 2021 rubric considers the quality of the submitted work, including its development, reasoning, organization, sources, and language. Its strongest grade requires a complete, accomplished response to the assignment; minor imperfections can remain. Its lower bands distinguish limited weaknesses from substantial gaps. This informs the qualitative grade descriptions below. [Harvard College Writing Program, *Grading Rubric* (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/websites.harvard.edu/dist/e/139/files/2025/06/grading_rubric_harvard_college_writing_program_2021.pdf)
- **Brown: purpose and genre change the task.** Brown's nonfiction curriculum distinguishes audiences and subgenres, treats notebooks as part of a writing practice, and develops self-assessment and revision across levels. This supports separate profiles for the different things this blog does. [Brown Nonfiction Writing Program, curriculum map](https://english.brown.edu/nonfiction-writing-program)
- **Berkeley: assess the response and its development in context.** Its writing assessment considers understanding, focus, examples, reasoning, organization, and language; it also accounts for the limited drafting time. This supports recording the draft stage and reading conditions before judging. Berkeley uses independent readers, which inspires the optional second-reader check here. [Berkeley Writing Assessment, structure and scoring](https://writing.berkeley.edu/berkeley-writing-assessment/structure-and-scoring-assessment)
- **Berkeley: feedback should teach a next move.** Its teaching guidance favors selected patterns, specific strengths, and actionable revision priorities over exhaustive error marking. This informs the limits on feedback below. [Berkeley GSI Teaching Guide, grading essays](https://gsi.berkeley.edu/gsi-guide-contents/student-writing-intro/grading/)
- **Yale: practice through drafts and focused tasks.** Its teaching guidance combines a clear assignment purpose, short exercises, revision, and peer feedback. This informs the practice loop. [Yale Poorvu Center, teaching students to write good papers](https://poorvucenter.yale.edu/teaching-students-to-write-good-papers)

The key adaptation: an academic argument needs to establish and support a claim. A journal can succeed by preserving a particular moment. Apply the standard appropriate to the piece.

## Choose the task before grading

Record the intended reader, purpose, and stage: exploratory draft, revision, or publication pass. If the author has not stated the purpose, infer it provisionally from the text and say so. Resolve consequential ambiguity before assigning an overall grade.

| Profile | What success means | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily journal | Preserve something worth remembering about this particular day. | Selected observations, readable movement through events, the writer's recognizable attention. A small ordinary event is sufficient. Reflection can remain brief or open. |
| Reflective essay | Explore an experience until the reader sees something differently. | A live question or tension, developed scenes, interpretation, and a purposeful movement between experience and reflection. |
| Reading response | Think with or against something read, heard, or watched. | Accurate representation of the source and a developed response: agreement with reasons, an objection, an application, or a connection that gets tested. |
| Explainer or argument | Help a reader understand a mechanism or consider a defensible claim. | Necessary context, clear terms, examples, reasoning, appropriate sources, and meaningful limits or alternative explanations. |
| Reference notes | Preserve and organize ideas for later retrieval. | Accuracy, attribution, useful grouping, and enough context to recover the idea. Original interpretation is optional unless promised. |

A mixed piece can use one dominant profile and a brief note about the secondary purpose. Use the same profile and stage when comparing drafts over time.

## Protect the voice and the truth

- Treat deliberate lowercase, conversational fragments, regional English, code-switching, and plain vocabulary as available stylistic choices. Assess whether the reader can follow them and whether they serve the piece.
- Distinguish three kinds of feedback: **error** (a demonstrable slip), **clarity issue** (a specific reader difficulty), and **preference** (another legitimate way to write it). Show the exact passage and explain the effect.
- Reward deliberate rhythm and specificity. Evaluate complexity by the thinking it enables, rather than by vocabulary, sentence length, or formality.
- Keep memories, dialogue, sensory details, motives, relationships, and personal facts grounded in the author's account. When development needs information that is absent, ask the author for it. Mark any illustrative invented example as invented.
- Keep source quotations exact and attribute borrowed ideas. Verify consequential external claims. Confirmed material misrepresentation lowers Specificity and support, and also Thinking and reflection when it breaks the reasoning; explain those distinct effects. Unresolved factual questions remain flags pending verification, rather than assumed errors.
- Treat unresolved feelings and uncertainty honestly. A personal essay may deepen a question without solving it. A daily log needs neither a dramatic arc nor a moral.
- Award no points for prestige, suffering, exceptional experiences, or intimate disclosure. An ordinary walk can be beautifully observed.

An error repeated throughout a piece is one learning pattern with several examples, not a reason to deduct points again under every criterion. Score its effects in the relevant criterion; explain any distinct effect elsewhere.

## The six criteria

Score each applicable criterion from **1 to 5** using the anchors below. **2** sits between the 1 and 3 descriptions: the basic skill appears, but the larger difficulty remains. **4** consistently meets 3 and shows substantial qualities of 5, with a specific remaining limitation. **5** means exceptional control for this piece's purpose, not perfection.

| Criterion | 1 — not yet working | 3 — functional | 5 — accomplished |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose and reader orientation** | The reader cannot tell what to attend to, or essential context is missing. | The subject and intention are understandable, though the central thread sometimes fades. | The reader knows what to follow and has enough context to care. The piece fulfills an interesting, appropriately bounded intention. A journal can do this through selection rather than an explicit thesis. |
| **Specificity and support** | Labels, generalities, or unsupported assertions do most of the work. | Relevant details or examples establish the event or idea, but some remain interchangeable or unexplored. | Particular details, scenes, examples, or evidence make the piece persuasive or memorable. Each earns its space by revealing something; abundance alone earns no extra credit. |
| **Thinking and reflection** | The piece repeats an idea or feeling where its stated purpose requires interpretation. | It explains some reasons or implications, but a key assumption, tension, or connection remains easy or unexamined. | The writer follows a difficulty far enough to produce an earned perspective. Reasons, uncertainty, and plausible alternatives receive appropriate attention. The reader encounters a mind discovering or testing something. |
| **Structure and movement** | Events or ideas are hard to place; paragraphs compete; essential steps are missing. | The route is mostly followable, with some repetition, abrupt shifts, or uneven pacing. | Ordering, paragraph boundaries, transitions, and pacing help the reader move through the piece. The ending feels appropriate to its purpose: a precise stopping point can work as well as a conclusion. |
| **Voice and sentence craft** | Generic, inflated, or imprecise wording obscures the writer's attention; sentence habits impede reading. | The voice is recognizable and mostly clear, though some phrases or sentence patterns feel habitual. | Word choice and rhythm feel specific to this writer and this material. Sentences vary as the thought requires. Compression, repetition, and fragments feel chosen; directness and ambiguity are used with control. |
| **Mechanical control** | Recurring slips frequently obstruct meaning or force the reader to reconstruct sentences. | Meaning remains accessible, with occasional distracting errors or unintentional shifts. | Grammar, spelling, reference, punctuation, and time frames support an easy reading. Remaining slips are minor; intentional language choices remain intact. |

For a criterion that the declared task genuinely does not require, mark **N/A** and explain why. Reference notes can omit independent reflection. A fragment supplied for review cannot support an overall structural judgment. Missing evidence about a criterion is a reason to withhold that score, not to guess a low one.

## Weight the criteria by purpose

These weights are proposed practice priorities, not research findings or university percentages. Choose the profile before seeing the scores. Each column totals 100.

| Criterion | Journal | Reflective essay | Reading response | Explainer / argument | Reference notes |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Purpose and reader orientation | 10 | 15 | 10 | 20 | 15 |
| Specificity and support | 25 | 20 | 20 | 25 | 30 |
| Thinking and reflection | 15 | 25 | 30 | 25 | 0 |
| Structure and movement | 15 | 20 | 15 | 15 | 30 |
| Voice and sentence craft | 30 | 15 | 15 | 10 | 15 |
| Mechanical control | 5 | 5 | 10 | 5 | 10 |

Optional progress index:

```text
weighted mean = sum(active weight × criterion score) / sum(active weights)
```

Report it to one decimal place, for example **3.7/5**, with the profile and any N/A criteria. Remove N/A weights from both sums. A zero-weight criterion contributes nothing. Withhold an overall index if the piece's purpose, support, or structure cannot be assessed, or if no positive-weight criterion can be scored. Compare only compatible profiles, stages, and active criteria; small score changes are not proof of progress.

For a letter-style judgment, use these local descriptions rather than converting the mean mechanically:

| Band | Judgment of this draft |
| --- | --- |
| **A range — distinction** | Complete for its purpose, strong throughout, and exceptional in several high-priority areas. Explain its distinction beyond correctness. |
| **B range — substantial success** | Substantial achievement with one consequential weakness or several smaller gaps. |
| **C range — basic success** | The basic purpose is met, but important promises of the task remain undeveloped. |
| **D range — purpose not yet achieved** | Essential missing context, development, or intelligibility prevents the piece from functioning. Length alone is not the issue. |
| **Unassessable** | Available text or context cannot support an overall judgment. Give bounded feedback instead. |

Explain any mismatch between the numeric index and the holistic band. Record unresolved accuracy or attribution concerns alongside the result. Give a grade to the submitted draft; record improvement separately. These bands borrow the idea of qualitative distinctions from Harvard's rubric; their application to this blog is a personal calibration.

## Examples from the archive

These calibrate individual criteria, not the overall grades of the posts. The excerpts are quoted from the archive; proposed wording is an exercise, not an applied edit.

**A detail worth preserving.** In [Turning 22](../posts/22.md), the memory includes “eating digestive biscuits with hot soy milk” while reading in the living room at midnight. For **Specificity and support**, this is evidence toward a 4: time, place, food, and activity recover a particular routine. A 5 would also require examining how the whole piece selects and uses its details. The next practice move is to find another equally observed moment, rather than decorating this sentence with more adjectives.

**A local mechanical weakness.** In [I got braces today](../posts/braces.md):

> As he was progressed patiently teeth by teeth, and methodically completed each sections of my teeth, I slowly realize this wasn't going to be easy.

For this passage, **Mechanical control** is around 2: the event is recoverable, but “was progressed” and “each sections” make the reader repair the construction. One possible revision is:

> As he worked patiently, tooth by tooth, I slowly realized this wasn't going to be easy.

That revision improves local control; it earns no automatic increase in the post's thinking or structure scores. Its usefulness must also be checked against the surrounding time frame and the author's preferred rhythm.

## Review protocol for the writer and other readers

1. **Set the task.** Record the path or title, date/version, intended reader, purpose, profile, and draft stage. State any assumptions. Completion: the review has a declared target rather than an imagined school assignment.
2. **Read for meaning first.** Read the whole supplied piece before marking errors. Write a one-sentence account of what it does. Completion: the account can be checked against the text.
3. **Score with evidence.** For every applicable criterion, give a score, an exact excerpt or paragraph reference, the reader effect, and what separates this draft from the next anchor, or why the highest anchor applies. Ground absence claims too: identify the relevant section and what it leaves unanswered. Completion: every score has a textual rationale; N/A and uncertainty are explicit.
4. **Choose priorities.** Identify two specific strengths to retain and at most two revision priorities. One priority may concern meaning or form; the other may concern clarity or a recurring mechanical pattern. Select by effect on the chosen purpose, not by ease of proofreading. Completion: each priority names a passage and a practicable action.
5. **Assign one exercise.** Give a 10–20 minute task on a paragraph or short passage. Include a visible success condition. Offer an illustrative edit only when requested or useful to explain a point; distinguish it from the author's original. Completion: the writer can attempt the exercise without having an agent rewrite the whole piece.
6. **Revisit the result.** Compare the revision with the original using the same criteria and profile. Explain what improved, what weakened, and what remains uncertain. Ask the writer which version sounds more like them. Completion: the next practice target follows from an observed difference, rather than a higher number alone.

The feedback limits follow [Berkeley's guidance on focused, formative comments](https://gsi.berkeley.edu/gsi-guide-contents/student-writing-intro/grading/). The draft-and-practice cycle is adapted from [Yale's teaching guidance](https://poorvucenter.yale.edu/teaching-students-to-write-good-papers).

If a second reader is available, have them score independently before seeing the first review. For a disagreement of two or more points on a criterion, compare the passages and anchor interpretations before seeking a consensus. Do not average away a meaningful disagreement. Agent scores remain fallible judgments; the evidence and the writer's response matter more than numerical agreement.

## A sustainable practice loop

Choose one piece a week for deliberate review; everyday journaling can remain exploratory. Self-assess before requesting feedback. Record the review date, piece, profile, one target, what you tried, and what changed in whatever practice log you already use. Create a new log only when wanted.

Rotate exercises according to the current weakness:

| Target | Exercise | Observable result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More particular observation | Choose one abstract label such as “a good day.” Recover a real action, object, or exchange that led to it. | A reader can identify what made this occasion distinct. All added details come from memory or records, with uncertainty acknowledged. |
| More developed reflection | Take one source idea or confident conclusion. Add why you accept it, one complication, and what you now think. | A reader can distinguish the borrowed idea, your reasoning, and the remaining question. For reference-only notes, practice attribution instead. |
| Clearer movement | Give each paragraph a short label for its job; inspect repetitions, missing steps, and order. | A reader can follow why one paragraph leads to the next. Keep or rearrange paragraphs for a stated reason. |
| More controlled sentences | Read one paragraph aloud. Mark where the thought restarts, then test a different boundary or word order. | Compare both versions: the intended relationships are clearer and the rhythm still feels like yours. Shorter is not automatically better. |
| Fewer recurring slips | Choose one error family, such as agreement. Find the subjects and verbs in one paragraph, then check a different paragraph without help. | You can identify and explain the pattern in fresh text, rather than only accept corrections supplied by a reviewer. |

The paragraph-label exercise adapts [Harvard Writing Center's reverse outlining](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/tips-organizing-your-essay), which examines a draft's existing order rather than assuming its structure is already right.

Once a month, compare a few pieces of similar purpose, length, and draft stage. Look for a skill appearing in fresh work without a prompt. Preserve passages where clarity and personal voice coexist. Change a rubric weight or anchor only for a stated reason; date the change and avoid treating scores under different versions as directly comparable.
